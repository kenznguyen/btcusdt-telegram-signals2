## 2. Open-decision matrix

### D82-A — Recovery model

| Candidate | Advantages | Risks / costs |
|---|---|---|
| Provider-native multi-party recovery | Fewer bespoke secret-handling components; provider may enforce quorum | Provider lock-in; provider admin compromise and account recovery become critical |
| Independent escrow authority | Separates recovery from primary provider account | Adds another trust domain, custody cost and operational dependency |
| Threshold-controlled recovery material | No single custodian holds complete recovery capability | Ceremony complexity, share-loss risk, difficult rotation and disaster drills |

**Discussion default:** compare provider-native quorum and independent escrow first; do not design a custom threshold scheme without demonstrated need and specialist review.

**Owner decision (2026-11-04): APPROVED.** Select **Independent escrow authority** as the recovery model, combined with the primary KMS/HSM for routine operations. Set the accepted loss-of-recovery risk to **LOW** (prioritize recoverability, accepting additional controls and operational cost). The recovery capability must be administratively and trust-domain independent from the primary KMS/HSM account.

At least two independent approvers are required; requester and executor are excluded from approving the same recovery. This constraint is part of the D82-A approval; exact roster, two-of-N quorum, succession and emergency exception remain open under D82-B. No vendor, escrow custodian, jurisdiction, account or implementation is selected.

### D82-B — Approval quorum and role separation

**Owner decision (2026-11-04): APPROVED — Option B, 2-of-3 role-based quorum.**

- Three independent trust-domain roles: system-owner delegate, independent escrow custodian delegate, independent security/recovery delegate.
- Two distinct people from distinct trust domains must approve the exact recovery case.
- Requester and executor are excluded from the counted quorum.
- Pre-designated alternates are permitted only when designated/governed before an incident; no ad hoc enrollment or role transfer during an active case.
- No emergency bypass below quorum. If quorum cannot be assembled, the case remains `BLOCKED_QUORUM_UNAVAILABLE`.
- GitHub review, chat/verbal approval or executor assertion is not a substitute for authenticated, case-bound approval.

Decision record: `docs/evidence/P5.4.82B-APPROVAL-QUORUM-AND-ROLE-SEPARATION-OPTIONS-2026-11-04.md`.

**Still open before implementation:** actual roster and identity proofing; alternate mapping/activation authority; evidence of distinct trust domains; credential lifecycle/revocation; case-bound approval protocol and audit; conflict-of-interest handling; quorum availability monitoring. No people, legal entities, providers, jurisdictions or accounts are selected.

### D82-C — Custody jurisdiction and account ownership

**Status: DEFERRED — LEGAL/OWNERSHIP FACTS REQUIRED; models A/B/C not selected.**

Three models are compared:
- **A:** one legal owner/provider organization with separated accounts/projects — simplest, but organization-level and legal common-mode risks remain.
- **B:** one legal owner with primary KMS/HSM and escrow in separate provider organizations/accounts — stronger technical/admin separation, but common legal-owner and jurisdiction dependencies remain.
- **C:** separate legal entities and provider organizations/accounts — potentially strongest legal/admin separation, with substantially higher legal, tax, compliance and operating complexity.

**Owner direction (2026-11-04):** keep the choice among A/B/C on hold until the legal owner and applicable legal conditions are established. This is deferral only; it does not approve A/B/C. Legal ownership, contracting authority, jurisdiction, residency/export/retention constraints and account-admin boundaries remain unresolved. Do not infer the user's country, corporate status or eligible contracting entity.

Minimum design principles: escrow must not share the primary KMS/HSM privileged account/admin group; separate privileged identities and authentication roots; document data/key ownership and recovery rights; inventory residency separately for ciphertext, manifest, audit, replicas and processing; establish provider exit and recovery paths.

**Owner decisions required:** (1) ownership model or defer; (2) legal data owner and authority; (3) primary KMS/HSM account owner; (4) escrow custodian/account owner; (5) permitted jurisdictions/regions by data class; (6) required independence across legal entities, provider organizations and identity providers; (7) qualified legal/compliance review gate.

Decision proposal: `docs/evidence/P5.4.82C-CUSTODY-JURISDICTION-AND-ACCOUNT-OWNERSHIP-PROPOSAL-2026-11-04.md`.

Unknown ownership/jurisdiction/data location => `BLOCKED_D82C_UNRESOLVED`; unproven primary/escrow admin separation => `BLOCKED_TRUST_BOUNDARY`. No provider selection or provisioning is authorized.

### D82-D — Audit sink and tamper evidence

| Candidate | Advantages | Risks / costs |
|---|---|---|
| Independent append-only managed log | Operationally simpler | Trust depends on provider/admin controls |
| WORM/object-lock evidence store | Strong retention controls | Retention configuration and legal holds are operationally sensitive |
| Independently anchored hash chain | Detects alteration relative to external anchor | More components; anchoring cadence and availability must be specified |

**Discussion default:** independent append-only destination with immutable retention controls and a separately held integrity anchor. This is a design candidate, not a provider selection.

**Owner must decide:** sink ownership, retention, replication/failure domain, tamper-evidence mechanism, audit availability requirement and privacy controls.

### D82-E — KEK rotation and historical DEK handling

| Candidate | Advantages | Risks / costs |
|---|---|---|
| Rewrap DEKs under new KEK version | Keeps historical payload ciphertext unchanged | Requires secure unwrap/rewrap workflow and durable audit |
| Retain old KEK versions for historical decrypt | Avoids mass rewrap | Long-lived key dependency and compromise exposure |
| Re-encrypt payloads | Can retire old encryption dependencies | Expensive, creates new backup objects and consistency risks |

**Discussion default:** immutable key versions and audited DEK rewrap, while preserving the original encrypted payload object and its digest. Re-encryption is not the default and needs separate migration controls.

**Owner must decide:** rewrap eligibility, old-version retention, compromise response and whether re-encryption is ever allowed.

### D82-F — Signing-key rotation and revocation semantics

| Candidate | Advantages | Risks / costs |
|---|---|---|
| Historical verification by recorded trusted key version | Supports long-term offline verification | A compromised key may have signed malicious manifests during its validity |
| Immediate global distrust on compromise | Strong containment | May make legitimate historical backups unverifiable |
| Time-bounded trust plus incident-scoped revocation | More precise | Requires trustworthy signing-time evidence and incident adjudication |

**Discussion default:** versioned public-key trust records with validity intervals and explicit incident-scoped revocation; never infer historical trust solely from current provider key status.

**Owner must decide:** trust-time evidence, compromise cut-off policy, treatment of signatures with uncertain signing time and archival verification horizon.

### D82-G — Workload identity federation and token lifetime

| Candidate | Advantages | Risks / costs |
|---|---|---|
| Short-lived federated workload identity | Avoids static credentials | Depends on identity provider, audience binding and federation configuration |
| Short-lived issued credentials from a broker | Central policy point | Broker becomes critical infrastructure |
| Static service credentials | Simple to integrate | High leakage and rotation burden; not recommended for privileged production use |

**Discussion default:** short-lived, audience-bound workload identity; no long-lived production secrets in APKs, repository secrets or CI artifacts.

**Owner must decide:** identity issuer, federation trust, token lifetime, revocation latency, device/workload attestation requirements and outage behavior.

### D82-H — Recovery RPO/RTO and service outage policy

| Candidate | Advantages | Risks / costs |
|---|---|---|
| Strict availability target | Faster recovery expectations | Higher redundancy and operational cost |
| Conservative recovery target | Lower cost and fewer dependencies | Longer period with backups inaccessible |
| Tiered target by incident severity | Aligns cost with criticality | More policy and test complexity |

**Discussion default:** set measurable RPO/RTO only after inventorying backup cadence, key-service dependencies, independent audit availability and human approval coverage. Do not invent numerical targets.

**Owner must decide:** numerical RPO/RTO, outage escalation, maintenance windows and acceptable key-service unavailability.

### D82-I — Android/JVM cryptographic implementation constraints

| Candidate | Advantages | Risks / costs |
|---|---|---|
| Approved platform/JVM crypto provider | May reduce bundled code | Behavior varies by Android/API/runtime version and provider |
| Audited portable crypto library | More predictable algorithm/API surface | Dependency, update, native-code and supply-chain risks |
| Remote cryptographic operations for all sensitive actions | Keeps key custody remote | Network dependency and latency; does not remove local plaintext DEK handling during encryption |

**Discussion default:** do not select a library until the minimum Android API, JVM/runtime matrix, supported cipher parameters, nonce generation, key zeroization limitations, FIPS/compliance needs and maintenance posture are documented and tested.

**Owner must decide:** supported Android/runtime range, library qualification bar, compliance constraints and acceptable managed-memory limitations.

### D82-J — Wrapped-DEK metadata and canonical manifest contract

**Required fields to evaluate:** wrapping algorithm/profile ID, immutable KEK key-version ID, wrapped-DEK bytes or immutable reference, wrapped-DEK length and digest, Backup ID binding, AAD version, adapter contract version and recovery-policy reference.

**Risks:** putting provider-specific mutable aliases in the manifest; ambiguous byte encoding; signing different logical representations; allowing key metadata to be rewritten without authenticated manifest replacement.

**Discussion default:** versioned provider-neutral metadata with exact immutable key-version reference, deterministic canonical encoding and explicit AAD binding. The exact field names, binary representation and serialization are not approved by this matrix.

**Owner must decide:** field schema, canonical encoding, inline-vs-referenced wrapped-DEK representation and compatibility/migration policy.

## 3. Cross-cutting invariants

All options must preserve:
1. No plaintext DEK or private signing material in repository, APK, logs, manifests, CI artifacts or ordinary database records.
2. Destination credentials cannot decrypt or sign; signer cannot decrypt; recovery approver cannot execute restore alone.
3. Every sensitive operation is purpose-bound, scope-bound, authenticated, auditable and replay-resistant.
4. Uncertain provider outcomes remain uncertain until reconciled; never blindly repeat non-idempotent operations.
5. No plaintext/local-key fallback, alternate database writer or silent provider-account fallback.
6. KMS/HSM recovery does not imply D6 restore readiness, Recovery Latch release, Kill Switch clearing or writer admission.
7. D3/D4/D5 snapshot, migration, digest and domain verification remain mandatory.
8. CI uses synthetic keys and fake providers, and must be technically isolated from production endpoints.

## 4. Required evidence before a provider decision

Before comparing vendors or provisioning anything, collect:
- Android/JVM platform support matrix for AES-256-GCM and Ed25519, including exact nonce/tag/AAD behavior.
- Provider documentation for immutable key versions, wrap/unwrap authorization, signing, audit export, quorum controls, revocation and destruction.
- Independent account/admin separation and recovery-account behavior.
- Regional availability, data residency, export and retention constraints.
- Pricing/cost model for key operations, audit retention, immutable storage and recovery drills.
- A threat-model review of provider administrator compromise, identity-provider compromise, quorum collusion, account lockout and provider outage.
- A tested migration/exit plan that preserves historical decryptability and signature verification.

No vendor comparison can be considered complete without these sources and evidence.

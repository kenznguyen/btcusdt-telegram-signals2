## 1. Executive determination

Public evidence supports three TSA candidates for further diligence, but it does not justify selecting one:

1. **GlobalSign** has the strongest publicly documented fit for a general-purpose timestamp use case among the reviewed sources: its Timestamping Practice Statement defines non-qualified RFC 3161 timestamps for general use, publishes policy OIDs, states one-second accuracy against UTC, identifies SHA-256/SHA-384/SHA-512, describes GNSS plus UTC(k)-referenced NTP monitoring, publishes issuer verification and OCSP status procedures, and states an availability aim. However, the practice statement is dated 2022, and current endpoint/profile/contract/certificate chain/accuracy evidence must be reconfirmed.
2. **DigiCert** publishes an RFC 3161 endpoint and current timestamp certificate-chain materials. Its support page records certificate replacement on 2026-09-03 and a certificate update cycle of at least every 15 months. The reviewed page does not state a numeric accuracy bound, TSA policy OID, general-use scope, nonce behavior or a service-level commitment. A page heading/body typo says “RFC 3151” for the URL despite the page and surrounding description saying RFC 3161; clarify with the provider before relying on it.
3. **Sectigo** publishes an RFC 3161 endpoint and CPS details about clock synchronization and auditable clock changes. But its CPS explicitly says the TSAs are intended only for signing software in conjunction with a Sectigo Code Signing Certificate. This is a material scope restriction; it is not currently a suitable documented candidate for arbitrary BTCUSDT event/ledger timestamping without a written scope confirmation or different service contract.

**No provider is recommended or selected by this assessment.** GlobalSign is only the strongest *documentary candidate for follow-up diligence* based on the public documents reviewed, not an approved production choice or proven interoperable provider.

## 2. Standards baseline

### 2.1 RFC 3161

RFC 3161 requires a TSA to use a trustworthy time source, include a trustworthy time value, assign unique token serial numbers, identify the policy under which the token was created and timestamp a hash representation of the datum. The requester must validate the response, token signature, TSA certificate identity, message imprint and hash algorithm. The TSA certificate must carry the critical timestamping EKU.

The token's `genTime` is UTC. Optional `accuracy` represents deviation around genTime; if absent, accuracy may be available through another source such as the TSA policy. RFC 3161 explicitly allows the policy to contain conditions of use and availability of a timestamp-token log. These facts make the TSA's current policy/practice statement and durable historical validation materials essential, not optional procurement paperwork.

### 2.2 OCSP is certificate-issuer-bound, not a generic independent service choice

The OCSP URL is normally discovered from the target certificate's Authority Information Access (AIA) extension. RFC 6960 requires the client to verify the response signature, signer identity and authorization, certificate identity, and freshness using `thisUpdate` and, when present, `nextUpdate`. A delegated responder must be authorized by the issuing CA. The `good` status does not by itself prove that a certificate was ever issued or that the response time lies within the certificate validity period.

Therefore, do not choose a standalone OCSP vendor independently of the TSA certificate chain. For each TSA signer and issuing CA:
- retrieve and preserve the exact certificate and its AIA/CRL Distribution Points;
- verify the responder signer is the issuer or a correctly delegated responder;
- preserve the signed OCSP response/CRL, chain, retrieval time and all four status times where present;
- verify response freshness and authorization independently;
- retain historical evidence needed after responder or certificate rotation.

An online OCSP query made later cannot by itself recreate the status state at an earlier historical instant. A current `good` result is not proof of historical validity at `genTime`.

### 2.3 OCSP nonce

RFC 9654 updates RFC 6960 and supersedes RFC 8954. It allows nonce values up to 128 octets, requires an RFC 9654 requester to use at least 32 octets, requires supporting responders to accept 16–32 octets, and permits responders to omit the response nonce for request lengths 1–15 or 33–128 octets. Such omission is not nonce-echo binding; the replay/freshness property then needs another explicitly approved mechanism or remains NOT VERIFIED.

### 3.1 GlobalSign evidence details

The GlobalSign Timestamping Practice Statement (v1.0, 2022-02-10) publishes:
- non-qualified Timestamp Policy OID `1.3.6.1.4.1.4146.2.3` (generic);
- code-signing Timestamp Policy OID `1.3.6.1.4.1.4146.2.3.1.2`;
- Qualified Timestamp Policy OID `1.3.6.1.4.1.4146.2.5`;
- stated accuracy of one second against UTC;
- GNSS time signal with NTP monitoring against UTC(k) laboratory sources;
- SHA-256, SHA-384 and SHA-512 as listed timestamp digest algorithms;
- published TSU/issuing CA certificates and OCSP responder availability for revocation status;
- availability aim of 99% annual availability while reaching 99.95% average annual availability; it does not guarantee 100%.

The statement also says timestamp time is the processing moment, not the request-acceptance moment. This distinction must be preserved in any ingestion design. The published policy is not proof that the current endpoint emits the exact desired token profile or that the current service still operates under unchanged terms.

### 3.2 DigiCert evidence details

DigiCert's current Knowledge Base page lists the RFC 3161 endpoint and certificate chain material. It specifically records replacement of three timestamping certificates on 2026-09-03 and says timestamping certificates are updated at least every 15 months. This is useful evidence that certificate rollover is operationally relevant and must be handled as a routine lifecycle event, not an exceptional manual patch.

The page does not disclose the time accuracy, applicable policy OID, long-term validation evidence/log availability, OCSP nonce response behavior or service-level commitment needed for this project's V07 decision. These are questions for a written provider response, not assumptions.

### 3.3 Sectigo evidence details

Sectigo's Code Signing CPS (v1.0.0, effective 2024-08-26) says its two RFC 3161 TSAs are intended only for software signing when used with a Sectigo Code Signing Certificate. It documents clock synchronization with a UTC(k) source at least every 24 hours, leap-second synchronization, drift/jump detection and audit events for clock changes of one second or greater.

Those clock controls are useful public evidence, but the use restriction prevents treating the endpoint as generally eligible for unrelated event-ledger timestamps. Do not infer broader usage permission from the existence of a public URL.

## 4. OCSP assessment and correct architecture

There is no independent OCSP-provider shortlist in this document. The relevant OCSP service is the one authorized for the actual TSA signer/issuer certificate chain.

The implementation-neutral evidence contract is:
1. Parse AIA and CRL Distribution Points from the exact TSA signer and issuer certificates.
2. Resolve the OCSP responder from AIA or approved local configuration.
3. Verify OCSP response signature and signer authorization under RFC 6960.
4. Verify CertID matches the exact certificate being checked.
5. Preserve `thisUpdate`, `nextUpdate`, `producedAt`, and `revocationTime` without conflating them.
6. Validate CRL/OCSP consistency and responder certificate status according to the selected trust policy.
7. Preserve raw signed responses, certificate chain, CRL, retrieval timestamp, validation result and digests for future historical verification.
8. Treat unknown, unauthorized, stale, unavailable, malformed or contradictory evidence as NOT VERIFIED / quarantine under the already-approved fail-closed direction.

The historical policy must separately define how evidence acquired after the alleged signing time can establish or fail to establish historical eligibility. No public OCSP standard makes a later current `good` response retroactively prove the certificate was valid at an earlier time.

## 5. Gaps requiring written provider answers

Before a provider can become an owner decision candidate, request written and versioned answers for:
- exact non-qualified service scope for arbitrary opaque data/event hashes (not only Authenticode/code signing);
- current RFC 3161 endpoint(s), transport requirements and supported request profiles;
- current TSA policy OID(s), CPS/TPS version and change-notification process;
- actual accuracy guarantee, measurement basis, uncertainty semantics and whether `accuracy` is populated in tokens;
- hash algorithms accepted and signature algorithms/key sizes used by TSA certificates;
- nonce echo behavior and rejection behavior for malformed/unsupported requests;
- token serial-number uniqueness and behavior across restart/failover;
- current certificate chain, rollover schedule, overlap strategy and historical chain publication;
- OCSP/CRL URLs, delegated responder authorization, response freshness, nonce behavior and retention/archive availability;
- long-term token verification and historical status evidence after certificate expiry/revocation;
- service availability commitment, incident notification, support, retention, price and contractual liability;
- jurisdiction, data processing and whether only a one-way imprint leaves the system.

No provider has answered these questions in this assessment.

## 6. Candidate-neutral E2 proposal prerequisites

A later E2 request must be a separate owner approval and must name:
- one candidate and exact published endpoint;
- exact TSA policy OID and immutable trust-chain snapshot;
- isolated disposable test identity and test-only data imprints;
- exact request profile and client/verifier versions;
- strict request count/rate, test duration and monetary cap;
- no secrets or private keys sent to provider;
- captured raw request/response/token/certificate chain/OCSP/CRL artifacts and SHA-256 digests;
- independent offline verification procedure;
- stop conditions for unexpected certificate, policy, algorithm, nonce, endpoint or response behavior;
- no writes to T0, no production event data, no canonical DB, no strategy/ledger state, no live orders;
- cleanup and retention rules.

An E2 success would prove only the named endpoint/profile behavior under that test window. It would not approve a production provider, trust policy, numeric SLO, historical acceptance policy or E3/E4 execution.
